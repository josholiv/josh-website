I thought this book started out a little boring and weak, but it got better and better as it went on. By the end, the story was so tense and thrilling that I could not wait to learn what would happen next. I loved the rich, biblical language that Umberto Eco used throughout to describe the setting (such as the various rooms of the abbey) and expound on themes and symbols without giving away too much plain detail, so as to render the meaning of events in the book abundantly clear and one-dimensional. A 1984 [New York Times article](https://www.nytimes.com/1984/10/14/books/how-i-wrote-the-name-of-the-rose.html) written by Eco himself details his process and reasons for writing the novel. In it, he explains why he intentionally kept the pacing slow at the beginning:

> "After reading the manuscript, my friends and editors suggested I abbreviate the first hundred pages, which they found very difficult and demanding. Without thinking twice, I refused, because, as I insisted, if somebody wanted to enter the abbey and live there for seven days, he had to accept the abbey's own pace. If he could not, he would never manage to read the whole book. Therefore those first hundred pages are like a penance or an initiation, and if someone does not like them, so much the worse for him. He can stay at the foot of the hill."
> 
> — Umberto Eco

To me, that's fair. While the beginning sections are too slow for my taste, I get where Eco was coming from, and his strategy ultimately worked on me. The novel really draws you into its world. Towards the middle, I felt like one of the characters, like I was a medieval monk inside the abbey, and Adso was personally recounting his version of the story to me. 

There are so many good quotes in this book, but one that stuck with me was this passage about the library:

> Until then I had thought each book spoke of the things, human or divine, that lie outside books. Now I realized that not infrequently books speak of books: it is as if they spoke among themselves. In the light of this reflection, the library seemed all the more disturbing to me. It was then the place of a long, centuries-old murmuring, an imperceptible dialogue between one parchment and another, a living thing, a receptacle of powers not to be ruled by a human mind, a treasure of secrets emanated by many minds, surviving the death of those who had produced them or had been their conveyors.

I was impressed by how Eco portrayed the abbey as a sort of character in and of itself—it was not just a building where the events of the novel took place, but a mysterious and supernatural force that slowly revealed its secrets to the protagonists as they explored its dark passageways, read from the books of the library, and spoke with the other monks. I'm a sucker for books and movies that use supernatural elements that are plausibly real to the characters experiencing them. Like filmmaker Robert Eggers said in an [interview with the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/film/2022/apr/10/the-northman-director-robert-eggers-interview) when describing his 2022 Viking film *The Northman*, phenomena that seem supernatural or implausible to, for example, a modern audience might be experienced differently by a historical figure or character:

> "There are no Viking atheists. There are Valkyries and giants and trolls, and sometimes people get put off that they’re in these sagas that are supposed to be naturalistic. And I’m like, yeah, but they believed that this was real. It’s naturalistic to them."
> 
> — Robert Eggers

This "groundedness" is one reason *The Name of the Rose* is so impactful—to the characters, the crime, the mystery, and the horrors always occur for a reason, be it some kind of turbulent spiritual storm, an act of God, the cunning of the devil, or the deceit, greed, or lust of man. However, as the novel goes on, the characters are unwittingly dragged through a whirlwind of heinous crimes that challenge their logic and their beliefs. Eco does an excellent job of exploring themes of faith, doubt, obedience, reason, and what it means to be human.

## Rating

This is a four-star book for me. I have some minor issues with it, but overall I really liked it and think it would be worth another read.
